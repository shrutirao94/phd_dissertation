\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\openingpara
{We continually sense, interpret, and respond to the indoor environments we inhabit.}
{Through these processes, spatial and environmental conditions take on meaning in relation to emotion, activity, prior experience, expectations, and social situations. Indoor experience is therefore constituted through relations among people, built environments, and the circumstances of everyday life~\cite{wright2003making,dourish2001action}. The character of a room depends not only on its dimensions, materials, temperature, light, and sound, but also on what is taking place within it, who else is present, what its occupants anticipate or remember, and what the space enables, constrains, or demands of them. An office may feel calm during solitary work and exposed during a difficult conversation. A quiet room may support concentration while making every movement, cough, and fragment of speech conspicuous.}

These experiences are increasingly mediated by interactive and computational systems~\cite{alavi2019introduction,wiberg2020interaction}.\sidenote{I use \emph{interactive indoor environments} in this dissertation to refer to indoor built environments in which spatial, environmental, and computational systems are brought into relation through sensing, automation, adaptation, feedback, or data-driven controls.}. Offices, universities, homes, and public buildings use sensing, automation, and data-driven control to regulate lighting, temperature, ventilation, access, sound, and other environmental conditions~\cite{saputra2023smart, alavi2017comfort}. Such systems operate through explicit interfaces, environmental adjustments, and background automation, participating in how people work, learn, rest, move, and socialise.

\section{The challenge of specifying human experience}
Human--Building Interaction, or HBI\sidenote{\citet{alavi2019introduction}'s HBI brings HCI into relation with architecture, building technologies, and everyday spatial experience. This thesis approaches HBI as both a domain of system design and a way of investigating how buildings are sensed, interpreted, and inhabited.}, provides the disciplinary context for addressing this development\cite{alavi2019introduction}. It extends Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) beyond individual devices and interfaces to consider rooms, buildings, environmental systems, infrastructures, and spatial practices as part of interaction\cite{alavi2017comfort, wiberg2008environment}\sidenote{This broad understanding of interaction encompasses direct input, screen-based control, spatial behaviour, environmental response, and peripheral awareness.}. From this perspective, buildings participate in interaction through their spatial organisation, environmental conditions, social arrangements, and computational systems. 

For this dissertation, HBI brings two connected concerns into focus. Human experience in built environments is complex, while the forms and relations encompassed by this complexity remain under-specified. HCI also offers theories, methods, and forms of design knowledge through which this complexity can be investigated and supported. Together, these concerns establish a need to clarify what forms of experience matter to HBI and how they can inform future interactive indoor environments.

Indoor environmental comfort has provided one of the principal frameworks for evaluating buildings from the occupant perspective. Comfort is commonly assessed through measurements of temperature, lighting, sound, and air quality, together with occupants' reports of satisfaction, preference, and perceived environmental quality. These approaches provide essential evidence about environmental performance, occupant wellbeing, and the usability of indoor spaces.

A broader account of indoor experience also requires attention to how environmental conditions are interpreted in relation to particular activities, social situations, and personal circumstances. A space may meet established performance standards while still feeling fatiguing, exposed, socially uncomfortable, or poorly aligned with the activity taking place. Occupants may accept environmental discomfort because a location provides privacy, familiarity, or proximity to colleagues. The experiential significance of a condition therefore depends on the situation in which it is encountered.

Research on buildings has increasingly addressed experiential concerns alongside indoor environmental comfort. These studies commonly focus on particular experiences, populations, technologies, or situations, while their relationship to a broader account of human experience remains less clearly articulated. Experience can consequently remain an expansive term without a clear account of the forms and relations it encompasses.

This produces three connected research problems. The first is
conceptual: what forms of human experience does research on interactive
indoor environments address, and how does it seek to shape them? The
second is methodological: how can HBI investigate experiences that are
contextual, relational, partly ambiguous, and not always easily
articulated, and how can the resulting knowledge inform design? The
third concerns future design: what experiential possibilities can guide
interactive indoor environments, and how can these possibilities be
developed and examined through concrete systems?

\section{an affective orientation for human--building interaction}
This dissertation advances \emph{affective HBI}---an orientation that
foregrounds affect in the investigation and design of interactive
indoor environments. Affect is often approached as an internal state that can be detected, classified, or predicted. Within this framing, a system may infer whether an occupant is calm, stressed, engaged, or frustrated and adjust its behaviour accordingly. This dissertation instead approaches affect as relational and situated.

Within this orientation, emotion can disclose how a person interprets
and relates to their surroundings. Calmness, for example, may be associated with relative quiet, daylight, or freedom from interruption, and may carry different meanings while working, resting, waiting, or observing others. An affective orientation retains these relations and examines calmness as a situated experience shaped by several conditions.

The dissertation develops affective HBI in two connected ways.
Methodologically, it examines how the complexity of lived indoor
experience can be investigated and articulated. As a design orientation,
it considers how interactive environments can support richer sensory,
emotional, social, and ecological forms of experience. These directions
are developed through Affective Interaction as a methodological lens,
multisensory biophilic design as a design approach, and an
AI-transformed indoor soundscape as a concrete design instance.

The overarching research question is:
\begin{quote}
How can an affective orientation enable Human--Building Interaction to specify and investigate the complexity of indoor experience and inform the design of future interactive indoor environments?
\end{quote}



This question is addressed through four research questions:

\begin{researchquestions}
    \researchquestion{scoping}{ What forms of human experience are addressed in research on interactive indoor environments, and how does this research seek to shape them through design and technology?}
    \researchquestion{afi}{What does an affective lens reveal about how occupants experience and interpret interactive indoor environments, and how can these insights inform HBI research and design?}
    \researchquestion{biophilic}{What possibilities does multisensory biophilic design offer for future interactive indoor environments, and how can these possibilities be articulated to support HBI design?}
    \researchquestion{sound}{How can an AI-transformed office soundscape be developed as a form of biophilic interaction, and what does an affective lens reveal about how it is experienced and interpreted in shared workplace contexts?}
\end{researchquestions}

\section{mapping human experience design research in interactive indoor environments}

A scoping review of 192 papers identifies 11 targeted human experiences,
20 design mechanisms, and two typologies of technological intervention:
seven intervention types and their placement across the temporal layers
of buildings. Together, these outputs provide a structured account of
what researchers have sought to support, the design approaches through
which they have pursued these experiences, and the technological forms
their interventions have taken.

The review establishes the conceptual foundation for the dissertation. It shows that research on interactive indoor environments engages with a range of experiential concerns, although these remain dispersed across distinct concepts, methods, and design strategies. For this dissertation, the review points to two productive directions: emotional experience as an entry point for investigating how indoor environments are lived and interpreted, and biophilic design as a comparatively underdeveloped approach for expanding the sensory and relational possibilities of future interactive environments. The following studies develop these directions through affective inquiry and multisensory biophilic design.

\section{investigating indoor experience through affective interaction}
Building on the review's identification of emotional experience as a productive entry point, the next study examines what becomes visible when indoor experience is approached through affect. It uses Affective Interaction as a methodological lens in two situated occupant studies: an in-situ survey comparing emotion- and comfort-framed accounts, and a building walk examining how occupants interpret and adjust to their surroundings.

The analysis identifies five Ways in which occupants experience and
interpret interactive technology in indoor environments, including
through bodily cues, anticipation, social interpretation, situated
adjustment, and sense-making around building automation. From these
empirical findings, the study develops six Affective Practices and six
corresponding Interaction Qualities. Together, these outputs articulate the bodily, anticipatory, social, and interpretive processes through which occupants establish relations with indoor environments. They inform HBI research by showing how situated methods can retain the conditions through which experience acquires meaning, and inform design through corresponding Interaction Qualities.

\section{expanding multisensory biophilic design possibilities}
The dissertation then turns from investigating lived indoor experience to considering the experiential possibilities of future interactive environments. Designing at the scale of indoor space involves spatial, material, sensory, and technological relations; this stage therefore engages architecture and spatial expertise. Multisensory biophilic design, also highlighted in the scoping review, offers a direction for considering how these relations might support encounters with nature and natural processes within technologically mediated indoor environments.\sidenote{Biophilic design is often represented through plants, daylight, water, and natural materials. This dissertation approaches it more broadly through the relations with nature and natural processes that spatial, material, sensory, and interactive qualities can support.}

Through semi-structured interviews with 13 architecture and spatial experts, the study examines what the legacy, practice, and imaginaries of biophilic architecture can contribute to interactive indoor environments. It develops eight themes reflecting expert imaginaries of biophilic futures and five design opportunities for technologically mediated indoor environments. The themes broaden biophilic interaction towards multisensory, interpretive, bodily, participatory, ecological, justice-oriented, and more-than-human forms of experience. The design opportunities indicate how interactive technologies might support these possibilities. Together, these outputs establish an expanded experiential and ecological scope for biophilic interaction.

\section{developing a biophilic interaction design framework}
The following study examines how these expanded possibilities can be
articulated through design. Three expert design sessions form a
progressive inquiry into multisensory biophilic interaction. The first
session identifies biophilic experiences diminished or absent indoors,
the second examines their spatial and material translation, and the
third focuses specifically on sound as a biophilic design material.
Across the sessions, architects and spatial designers translate
qualities associated with natural phenomena into interactive spatial
propositions. From this material, the study develops a biophilic interaction design
framework comprising seven biophilic dimensions, seven design values,
and six interaction forms.

The dimensions characterise the experiential design space, the values articulate the priorities and judgements that shape design decisions, and the interaction forms describe recurring ways in which occupants might engage bodily and spatially with technologically mediated environments. The framework provides HBI with a structured language for conceiving, comparing, and developing multisensory biophilic interactions.

The third session focuses specifically on sound and establishes the
design direction continued in the final study.

\section{an ai-transformed soundscape as a design instance}
The final study develops the Mediated Office Soundscapes System (MOSS)
as a form of responsive biophilic interaction for shared workplaces. It
continues the sound-focused direction established in the third expert
design session by transforming ongoing office sounds into nature-like
sounds in real time. Changes in typing, movement, speech, and other
activity therefore continue to shape what listeners hear.\sidenote{
Treating sound as a design material broadens acoustic design from the
reduction and control of disturbance towards the role that sonic
qualities can play in situated, multisensory experience.}

The biophilic studies inform the decision to treat sound as an
environmental and responsive element of indoor design. The affective
lens, operationalised through Affective Interaction, informs the
evaluation of how listeners perceive and interpret the relation between
office activity and transformed sound, how the system affects attention
and experience, and when it appears suitable for shared workplace
contexts.

MOSS introduces the concept of \emph{biophilic mediation}: transforming
sounds already occurring indoors into nature-like sounds while retaining
a perceptible relation to the incoming sound. An online listening study
compares office-only, conventional-nature, and AI-transformed conditions
across calm and active office scenes. Live prototype demonstrations with
11 experts examine the system's responsiveness, intelligibility, spatial
fit, configurability, and possible workplace use. Together, these
studies identify the experiential qualities, limitations, and design
conditions associated with an AI-transformed office soundscape.


\section{dissertation contributions}

The study-specific outputs developed across the dissertation are brought together in four contributions to HBI.

\begin{researchcontributions}
\researchcontribution{c1}{
The dissertation contributes a structured account of human-experience design research in interactive indoor environments. It clarifies the range of experiences addressed across the field and how these have been pursued through design mechanisms and technological interventions. In doing so, it specifies HBI's experiential agenda and establishes the conceptual foundation for the subsequent contributions.}

\researchcontribution{c2}{
The dissertation establishes Affective Interaction as a methodological lens for HBI. The resulting Affective Practices and Interaction Qualities provide a vocabulary for examining the bodily, anticipatory, social, and interpretive processes through which occupants experience and respond to interactive indoor environments.}

\researchcontribution{c3}{
The dissertation develops multisensory biophilic interaction as a design approach for technologically mediated indoor environments. It contributes an expanded experiential and ecological design space and a framework of biophilic dimensions, design values, and interaction forms for reasoning about relations among people, technologies, built environments, and nature at architectural scale.}

\researchcontribution{c4}{The dissertation contributes MOSS, an AI-transformed office soundscape developed as a form of responsive biophilic interaction. Through biophilic mediation, sounds occurring within the office are transformed into nature-like sounds while changes in surrounding activity continue to shape the output. An online listening study and live expert demonstrations provide empirical accounts of how the resulting soundscape is experienced and interpreted, and identify conditions concerning responsiveness, attention, intelligibility, spatial fit, configurability, and possible integration into shared workplaces.}
\end{researchcontributions}

Together, these contributions position affective HBI as an orientation
that connects the specification and investigation of indoor experience
with the development and examination of future interactive
environments.

\section{dissertation structure} 
The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows. Chapter~2 maps human-experience design research in interactive indoor environments. Chapter~3 investigates indoor experience through the lens of Affective Interaction. Chapters~4 and~5 develop multisensory biophilic design possibilities and a biophilic interaction design framework. Chapter~6 develops an AI-transformed office soundscape as a form of biophilic interaction and examines how it is experienced and interpreted in relation to shared workplace contexts. Chapter~7 synthesises the findings, develops their implications for HBI and HCI, and identifies priorities for future research.
